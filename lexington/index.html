How Lexington Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Routine maintenance is the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and yearly descaling for any tankless unit on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular service, a standard tank usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like rust-colored water, popping noises, or moisture around the base, it's smart to start planning a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and causes far less stress than an emergency swap with water spreading across the floor.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Take a spent anode rod: replacing it is a quick, affordable repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a flooded utility room.

Hot Water Headaches Lexington Homeowners Face

Around Lexington, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of sources. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. Once the protective anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Code is another piece. Swapping out an older heater often means bringing the install up to current code: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line piped to an approved spot, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
